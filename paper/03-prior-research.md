# Chapter 2: Prior Research

## 2.1 Food Environment and Health

The term "food environment" has a variety of concepts. It refers to the
accessibility, availability, and average price of food in a social community.
This area of study is important for understanding obesity rates, because it
is closely connected to eating habits and health outcomes. In a study from
2014, food option profiles were investigated in neighborhoods. The
researchers found that in neighborhoods where people lived further from the
supermarkets and the costs were higher for healthy foods like fresh
vegetables and fruits, people in those neighborhoods were more overweight
[1]. These neighborhoods, which have a poor chance of accessing healthful
food, were identified as "food deserts" in urban settings and showed a
greater prevalence of obesity than others, especially in lower-income
families. The researchers revisited the importance of environment and
structural discourse and strayed away from comments about personal
responsibility known as "willpower" to highlight the structures and
environment that limited choices around nutrition and affected lifestyle
disparities.

A systematic review included these primary findings and demonstrates that
neighborhoods with a high density of fast-food restaurants and only limited
access to healthy options consistently increased the risk of obesity [2].
Specifically, "obesogenic" environments are marked by the overabundance of
unhealthy food and limited physical activity opportunities and exist across
various contexts, cultures, and countries.

Children in particular are impacted by their food environment significantly.
Living in neighborhoods with high food density or attending schools near
clustered fast-food outlets is correlated with an increased incidence of
childhood obesity [3]. This issue continues to expand beyond obesity to other
diseases. "Food swamps" refer to people living in high-density residential
neighborhoods with plenty of fast-food restaurants, which have been
associated with all-cause mortality and obesity-related cancers [4]. To wrap
up, this body of literature illustrates how the importance of the food
environment is essential to understanding dietary behavior and health over
the long term.

## 2.2 Fast-Food Density, Accessibility, and Health Outcomes

There is a considerable relationship between neighborhood density of
fast-food restaurants and their proximity to where people reside. According
to one study, higher density and proximity to fast-food restaurants were
associated with a slightly higher prevalence of obesity and diabetes,
especially for those living in areas where there were limited parks and
playgrounds [5]. These studies use average measurements like fast-food
restaurant counts per square kilometer or miles and people's self-reported
driving or walking time to these locations. Other research has found that
even a small increase in the availability of unhealthy food can lead to a
significant increase in community average body mass index (BMI) levels [6].
The density and accessibility of fast-food restaurants are important factors
for understanding health outcomes and contribute to disparities.

Moreover, this study has also found a significant correlation between the
density of fast-food restaurants and their proximity. It indicates a clear
inequality in the community, as the minority and low-income families were
most likely exposed to these areas [5], [6]. These findings suggest that the
limited accessibility of safe parks and playground spaces where people can
do exercise, in combination with the high density and proximity to fast
food, ultimately affects the health of these communities disproportionately.

## 2.3 Rural–Urban and Socioeconomic Disparities

Research indicates that the prevalence of adolescent overweight and obesity
is greater in rural than urban settings in the U.S. [7]. Distance to grocery
stores, perceived insufficient transportation to healthy foods, and fewer
youth physical activity facilities are all shown to be associated with
obesity in rural adolescents [7]. These observations have been documented in
state and national policies, which provide useful evidence. For example,
low-income households are more likely to experience food insecurity, which
has been related to obesity in a report by the Food Research & Action
Center [8]. The U.S. Census Bureau has income and economic data that
indicates a substantial disparity in the median income of households at the
county level [9], and that these income disparities are related to diet,
nutrition, and other health outcomes. The Food Environment Atlas dataset
from the U.S. Department of Agriculture combines food environment, health
outcome, and socioeconomic indicators and allows analyses at the county
level [10].

## 2.4 National and Global Health Statistics

Reports from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention / National
Center for Health Statistics indicate that fast food is consumed by adults
of all ages in the United States, but is especially more often consumed by
younger adults, men, disabled adults, and adults with lower educational
attainment [11], [12]. These high rates of fast-food consumption are related
to the significant prevalence of diet-related diseases.

Integrating findings from other global studies, the World Health
Organization noncommunicable disease profiles for the United States and
other countries identify obesity as a common risk factor [13]. The World
Bank similarly aggregates a variety of health and income indicators which
show that health disparities, including dietary and nutrition outcomes, are
complex issues related to income inequality [14]. Unhealthy food
environments are therefore a public health issue countries are experiencing
around the world.

## 2.5 Virginia-Specific Research and Policy Reports

Focusing on Virginia tells a similar story yet adds context at the state
level. The Virginia Food Access Investment Fund annual report has
state-level data on the investment made to expand healthy food retail in
underserved areas [15]. Southwest Virginia has documented food security
problems where remoteness, poverty, and lack of competitive retail combine
[16].

The Virginia Department of Behavioral Health and Developmental Services
(DBHDS) agencies have identified nutrition vulnerabilities among at-risk
populations [17], and the Virginia Rural Health Plan has identified food
security as an important rural health issue [18]. As it relates to the
spatial component, the Virginia Association of Counties [19] includes
county boundary files and other governance-related information that may be
useful in accessing/using county-level data. SCHEV has state-level
education data that captures sharp county-level differences in college
degree attainment [20]. Taking into account the established association
between education, income, and health, gaps like this help explain
nutrition- and obesity-related differences.

## 2.6 Federal Research and Policy Reports

In addition to state programs, federal programs are directly addressing food
environmental inequities and health-optimizing communities as well. One
prominent example of this work is the Environmental Quality Incentives
Program (EQIP) High Tunnel Initiative of the Natural Resources Conservation
Service (NRCS) of the United States Department of Agriculture (USDA). This
program provides farmers with financial and technical assistance for the
development of high tunnels — structures which help farmers extend their
growing season, assist with protection from extreme weather variability, and
improve soil quality [21].

The High Tunnel Initiative example intentionally addresses barriers to
accessing fresh and affordable produce, especially for rural and underserved
communities without supermarkets. By improving local agricultural
production, it improves access to fruits and vegetables in place of
ultra-processed food. High tunnels further provide the ability to mitigate
climate variability and enhance crop yield, thereby reducing price
variability associated with fresh produce.

The High Tunnel Initiative positively expands upon Virginia's suite of
state-level policy programs, such as Virginia's Food Access Investment Fund,
to support a multi-scalar policy response. State-level policy programs are
largely limited to retail access or economic development, while the
Environmental Quality Incentives Program (EQIP) supports the ability of the
agricultural sector to deploy capacity prior to retail. Collectively, this
initiative provides an example of the collaboration of local, state, and
federal governments in multiple ways to promote food security and nutrition
equity.

## 2.7 Integration and Key Insights

In light of this, there are a few significant insights that need to be
emphasized.

**Accessibility and cost matter.** When healthy eating options are limited
or costly, obesity rates continue to ascend, regardless of age, geography,
and culture [1], [2].

**Location matters and contributes to inequities.** Racially-segregated
communities, lack of investment in neighborhoods, and rural isolation all
create barriers to healthy choices and safe venues for being active, which
lower rates of healthy outcomes [22], [7].

**Food insecurity is cyclical and endless like a Möbius strip.** Tight
budgets coupled with unreliable access to food force households to
ultra-processed cheap calories and ironically increase their risk for
obesity, while struggling with sustained access to food [23]. To cut off
the strip, policy is possible, but must be location-specific. Programmatic
and investment strategies via healthy-food retail programs (for example,
Virginia's healthy-food retail grant) could be positive first steps, but
will require some form of long-term sustained effort and a place-based,
large-scale agency to accomplish policy change [15].

**This is a global problem.** Regardless of where you live, as long as
unhealthy food is less expensive and easier to get than healthy food, the
variables will matter at national and global levels.

## 2.8 Behavioral Responses and Consumer Psychology

Even though spatial and economic aspects of food environments have been
well studied, mental and behavioral considerations are emerging as arguably
the most important determinants of food choice. Behavioral economics
research shows that individuals tend to have present-biased preferences,
preferring to have food now rather than weighing the later health outcomes
of the decisions they make today. This "time discounting" suggests that a
preference for easily accessible and calorie-dense foods is stronger in
lower-income people who experience chronic stress, or at least a diminished
decision bandwidth [24].

Building on this, exposure to targeted marketing and information overload
of fast-food commercials also shapes consumer attitudes and perceptions of
"normal" eating behaviors [25]. With experiments and surveys, children and
adolescents have reported craving and purchasing more excessive-calorie,
nutrient-poor food as a result of constant advertisement exposure;
therefore, educating and self-regulating aren't enough. In sum, behavioral
vulnerability coupled with logistical availability creates a stacked effect
that increases instances of obesogenic behavior. Given these
considerations, developing behavioral concepts into public health
nutrition policy will help define food-environment concerns in an
integrated context.

## 2.9 Data-Driven Approaches and Predictive Modeling

Recent advances in computational and data sciences have greatly aided
researchers in evaluating food environments and predicting health outcomes.
Researchers have engaged spatial machine-learning models such as random
forest regression and geographically weighted regression to assess each
food environment's most powerful predictors of obesity and food-insecure
counties [26]. Fine-grained, predictive obesity prevalence maps can be
produced with combined datasets from the CDC, USDA Food Environment Atlas,
and the American Community Survey to visualize regional clustering patterns
of obesity prevalence.

These methods can also be used to simulate policy interventions. For
example, increasing grocery store density can be estimated to reduce
county-level obesity prevalence slightly in some rural areas [27].
Machine-learning-based clustering has also been used to categorize counties
based on total socioeconomic and health characteristics, creating a
quantifiable framework for prioritizing policy action. This advanced
analytic three-prong approach captures the potential of data-driven public
health completely and meshes well with the computational aspects of this
capstone project.

## 2.10 Evaluating the Effectiveness of Food Access Programs

Many state and federal initiatives exist to increase access to food, but
fewer have been rigorously evaluated. The Healthy Food Financing Initiative
(HFFI), a collaborative effort of the U.S. Department of Treasury, USDA, and
HHS, started in 2011 to increase healthy retail in underserved communities.
Longitudinal evaluations point to improvement in the presence of retail,
but with limited effect in changing BMI or fruit-and-vegetable consumption
without nutrition education and community engagement [28].

Similarly, the CDC's *State Indicator Report on Fruits and Vegetables*
(2022) reports that states that offer nutrition education, have incentives
for local agriculture, and take an urban planning approach tend to show
greater improvements in dietary behavior [29]. The Robert Wood Johnson
Foundation, in a Healthy Communities Initiative, also finds that programs
that are successful often couple increasing access to physical food with
high-level policy support such as zoning reform and improving school
meals [30]. Evaluation approaches indicate that access alone is not
sufficient for longer-term impact, and multi-sector improvement and
coordination are needed.

## 2.11 Digital Literacy and Health Promotion Campaigns

In the present digital age, nutrition knowledge and behavior change are
increasingly delivered via online and mobile app spaces. For example,
public health campaigns that have used social media include the NIH
"MyPlate on the Go" and CDC "Fruits and Veggies — More Matters" programs,
both of which have demonstrated measurable change in dietary knowledge and
self-reported healthy eating among young adults [30].

Digital interventions further help to address access to nutrition, health,
and behavioral health information in rural and low-income communities by
providing culturally-appropriate public health nutrition content and
helping to increase participation in virtual community health challenges.
However, misinformation surrounding current diet trends on digital platforms
like TikTok and YouTube can complicate nutritional literacy [31]. Therefore,
integrating evidence-based digital health education, in conjunction with
local policy interventions, can improve overall capacity infrastructure for
healthy eating.
