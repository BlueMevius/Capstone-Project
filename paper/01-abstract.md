# Abstract

**Data-Driven Geographic and Socioeconomic Determinants of Fast-Food Density and Public Health Outcomes in Virginia**

Geonwoo Roh, BS
George Mason University, 2025
Capstone Project Director: Dr. Kent Miller

The proposed capstone project examines how fast-food restaurant density,
socioeconomic characteristics, and public health outcomes in Virginia's counties
are interrelated through multi-source data (OpenStreetMap, American Community
Survey, and County Health Rankings 2023). This project incorporates economic
and health indicators along with geospatially based data to identify potential
structural inequalities within the food environment. The density of fast-food
restaurants (measured per 10,000 people) for each Virginia county was calculated
and compared to other socioeconomic indicators such as median household income,
the degree of income disparity as expressed by the Gini Index, and the
percentage of people 18 or older who are obese. ANOVA, correlation, and linear
regression techniques were employed as statistical methods to determine whether
the characteristics of a county's economy and health indicators can help predict
the degree of fast-food density relative to other parts of the county, or
whether fast-food density and poor health indicators correlate.

The research demonstrated that there is **not** a statistically significant
association between fast-food restaurants nearby and median county income.
Nevertheless, the level of inequality of household income has demonstrated a
minor yet statistically significant relationship to fast-food restaurant
density. In other words, counties with more income disparity between their
residents tend to have higher fast-food density than those with less disparity.
Additionally, the density of fast-food restaurants alone did not provide
evidence as an independent community-level predictor of obesity rates in the
adult population in Virginia; therefore, we can reason that a community's
health is likely influenced by a greater range of social, environmental, and
health-related factors in addition to the availability of fast-food
restaurants, which correlates with the level of income disparity in that area.
This research supports the position that the structural socioeconomic
inequalities in a community shape the nature of the commercial food
environment and serves as an important foundation for future consideration of
policy initiatives aimed at improving nutritional equity and health in
Virginia.
