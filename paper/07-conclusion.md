# Chapter 6: Conclusion

Overall in this research study, the effect of fast-food restaurant density
on the average income level and adult obesity (county-level, 2023) was
evaluated to determine whether there were any links between these three
variables at the county level in Virginia. Many interesting discoveries
came from this research.

First, it was found that there were no significant relationships between
fast-food restaurant density and median household income. This shows that
fast-food restaurants may be located primarily in population-dense
commercial centers regardless of household income levels.

Second, it was determined that income inequality had a significantly
positive association with fast-food density. This means that the
inequalities occurring in communities are much more significant than income
level when determining the local food environment, which matches previous
studies emphasizing that structural inequalities affect how communities
obtain and use unhealthy foods.

Fast-food density does not predict obesity prevalence across Virginia
counties — including under smoothness and linearity assumptions. Therefore,
the diet–health–fast-food relationship does not depend solely on the
density of a population within the state, but rather upon several other
variables, including behaviors, environments, cultural influences, and the
way policies impact how a person accesses and consumes unhealthy food. The
availability of fast food may be perceived as a marker of obesogenic
environments, but the findings of this research indicate that this single
measure does not sufficiently explain the variance between populations'
health levels and obesity levels across the Virginia counties studied.

Most studies show that structural inequities in an individual community's
food system directly correlate with the overall health of a community.
Although researchers have primarily focused on the number of fast-food
outlets in a community, there may be other areas of consideration — such as
food insecurity, physical activity access, community zoning, and
community-culture-related behaviors — that need to be included when
examining health inequities in any particular community. If policymakers
wish to address health equity and improve health outcomes for rural
residents, then all policymakers must work collaboratively across sectors
to address multi-level inequities related to access to healthy foods and
services in their communities — not just by increasing access points to
food retail outlets.
