# Chapter 1: Introduction

## 1.1 Background

*An Analysis of the Correlation Between Fast-Food Restaurant Density, Economic
Status, and Health Effects in Virginia's Counties.*

This research analyzes the linkages between county-level adult obesity,
county-level income, county-level income inequality, and fast-food density in
Virginia. The researcher will use data from multiple sources (including the
USDA Food Environment Atlas) as well as data collected from the Virginia
Department of Health and the CDC to study the relationship between
county-level economic status and adult obesity rates, and to find out how
socioeconomic differences affect the local food environment and result in
health disparities. It will also focus specifically on the relationship
between income inequality and access to healthful foods. The study will
analyze the spatial clustering of fast-food restaurants, food deserts, and
food swamps in the state of Virginia, looking at both county-level aggregation
and county-level disaggregation data.

## 1.2 Main Problem

The purpose of this study is to investigate associations between
socioeconomic conditions and health in the Commonwealth of Virginia. In
particular, we are interested in associations between fast-food restaurant
density, county income levels, income inequalities, and health outcomes.

## 1.3 Significance

Understanding connections among fast-food proximity, socioeconomic
measurements, and community health outcomes can serve to alleviate chronic
health disparities in Virginia. Fast-food density has been widely
characterized as the environmental force that determines dietary quality and
obesity. Specifically, social communities with limited access to healthier
food options were affected deeply.

This study is relevant because it shows that the Gini indicator has a
statistically significant relationship using income inequality as an
independent variable for fast-food outlet density in counties across Virginia.
The research suggests that there are structural socioeconomic inequalities
that influence the commercial food environment, and therefore a differential
distribution of health risks. The findings are consistent with and add to the
existing research on food deserts and health inequities.

Furthermore, Virginia has a substantial amount of regional variation in
income, rates of obesity, and food access, which makes it a relevant case
study for better understanding localized public health issues. By layering
geospatial data from OpenStreetMap with socioeconomic indicators from the
American Community Survey (ACS) and health outcomes from the County Health
Rankings dataset, this study is addressing an important gap in the
literature. The use of multi-source data will also support the evidence base
for policy mechanisms to reduce dietary-related disease burden or
socioeconomic inequality.

In conclusion, the findings expand the understanding of structural inequality
in designing public health initiatives and add important information for
policymakers looking at health equity on a county level.
